# [[Survey modes]]
- Writing good survey questions is only one part of survey research.
    - We must also decide how respondents will receive and answer those questions.
    - There is no single best survey mode.
        - The best mode depends on the particular study.
            - This decision is called the survey mode.
- Surveys can be organized into two broad groups:
    - ```mermaid
    flowchart TD
        A[Survey modes] --> B[Interviewer-administered]
        A --> C[Self-completion]
        B --> D[Face-to-face survey]
        B --> E[Telephone survey]
        C --> F[Postal survey]
        C --> G[Online survey]
    ```
- In an [[interviewer-administered survey]], an interviewer asks the questions and records the answers.
    1. [[Face-to-face surveys]],
    2. [[Telephone surveys]],
- In a [[self-completion survey]], respondents read the questions and record their own answers.
    1. [[Postal surveys]], and
    2. [[Online surveys]].

<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Face-to-face surveys]]
- A trained interviewer meets the respondent in person.
- The interviewer:
    - Reads each question,
    - Records each answer,
    - Follows the required question order, and
    - Clarifies instructions when necessary.
<!-- slide-break -->
### Advantages of [[face-to-face surveys]]
- The interviewer can build rapport with the respondent.
- The interviewer can explain the task and clarify misunderstandings.
- The researcher has more control over:
    - Who answers the survey,
    - The order of the questions, and
    - The completeness of the questionnaire.
- Longer and more complex questionnaires are possible.
- Respondents with limited reading skills can still participate.
- Visual materials and complex response categories can be shown to respondents.
- Response rates are often higher than in other survey modes.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Disadvantages of [[face-to-face surveys]]
- They are expensive.
    - Interviewers must be hired, trained, supervised, and paid for travel time.
- They take more time to organize and complete.
- A geographically scattered sample increases travel costs.
- Interviewers may influence respondents' answers, which is called [[interviewer effect]].
- Respondents may hide sensitive or socially undesirable behavior, which is called [[social desirability bias]].
- Interviewer safety and access to homes can become practical problems.

<!-- slide-break -->
### [[Interviewer effect]]
- The presence or characteristics of an interviewer can affect the answers respondents give.
- Possible influences include the interviewer's:
    - Age,
    - Gender,
    - Race or ethnicity,
    - Accent,
    - Clothing,
    - Facial expressions, and
    - Tone of voice.
- Different interviewers may also read questions or record answers differently.
- These differences create [[interviewer variance]].

<!-- slide-break -->
### [[Social desirability bias]]
- Respondents may give an answer that appears respectable or socially acceptable.
- They may:
    - Overreport socially approved behavior, or
    - Underreport socially disapproved behavior.
- Social desirability bias can occur in any survey.
- It is often stronger when respondents give answers directly to another person.
- Sensitive questions may therefore work better in a private self-completion section.
- **To reduce the interviewer effect**:
    - Train interviewers to:
        - Read every question exactly as written,
        - Keep a neutral tone and expression,
        - Avoid approving or disapproving answers,
        - Use only standardized explanations, and
        - Record answers accurately.
    - Monitor interviewers and check their completed surveys.
    - Training can reduce the interviewer effect.
        - It cannot guarantee that the effect disappears.
<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Telephone surveys]]
- A trained interviewer asks questions by telephone.
- Most large telephone surveys use computer-assisted telephone interviewing.
- The computer can:
    - Display the next question,
    - Apply skip patterns,
    - Check whether an answer is valid, and
    - Record the response directly.
- Telephone surveys are interviewer-administered, but the interviewer and respondent do not see each other.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Advantages of [[telephone surveys]]
- They are usually faster and less expensive than face-to-face surveys.
- A geographically scattered sample can be reached without travel.
- Interviewers can clarify questions and encourage respondents to continue.
- Centralized interviewing makes supervision easier.
- Question order and skip patterns can be controlled.
- The absence of face-to-face contact may make some sensitive questions easier to answer.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Disadvantages of [[telephone surveys]]
- Respondents cannot see:
    - Maps,
    - Images,
    - Long lists, or
    - Complicated response categories.
- People can screen or reject unfamiliar calls.
- A telephone sampling frame may exclude or underrepresent some groups.
- The interviewer effect and social desirability bias are still possible.
- Questions and response categories must be short and easy to remember.
<!-- slide-break -->
    - !!! warning "Telephone questions are heard only once"
        - Respondents must listen, understand, remember the response categories, and answer.
        - A question that works on paper may be too complicated by telephone.
        - Long questions and long response lists should be shortened or divided.

<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Postal surveys]]
- A questionnaire is mailed to a sampled person or household.
- The respondent completes and returns it without an interviewer.
- The package usually includes:
    - A cover letter,
    - Clear instructions,
    - The questionnaire, and
    - A prepaid return envelope.
- Follow-up reminders and replacement questionnaires are often necessary.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Advantages of [[postal surveys]]
- They cost less than face-to-face surveys.
- A geographically scattered sample can be reached.
- There is no interviewer effect.
- Respondents can answer at a time that is convenient for them.
- Respondents can check personal or household records.
- Questions and response categories can be presented visually.
- Greater privacy may improve answers to sensitive questions.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Disadvantages of [[postal surveys]]
- Data collection is slow because mailing and follow-up take time.
- The questionnaire must explain itself.
    - No interviewer is present to clarify a confusing question.
- Respondents can skip questions or answer them out of order.
- The researcher cannot be certain that the sampled person completed the survey.
- People with limited reading or writing skills may be excluded.
- Returned questionnaires may require manual coding and data entry.
- Nonresponse can be high when respondents are not interested in the topic.

<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Online surveys]]
- Respondents complete a digital questionnaire without an interviewer.
- The survey may be distributed through:
    - Email invitations,
    - Text messages,
    - A website,
    - A course or workplace system, or
    - A survey panel.
- A link does not create a sample.
    - The researcher must still decide who should receive the invitation.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Advantages of [[online surveys]]
- Data collection can be fast and inexpensive.
- Large and geographically scattered samples can be contacted.
- Answers enter the dataset automatically.
    - This saves time and reduces data-entry errors.
- The survey can apply:
    - Automatic skip patterns,
    - Response validation,
    - Randomized question order, and
    - Personalized question wording.
- Images and other visual materials can be included.
- There is no interviewer effect.

<!-- slide-break -->
### Disadvantages of [[online surveys]]
- People without reliable internet access or digital skills may be excluded.
- Email invitations can be ignored, filtered, or treated as spam.
- The researcher may not know who completed the survey.
- Respondents may quit when the survey is long, slow, or difficult to use.
- Questions may appear differently across phones, tablets, and computers.
- Respondents may worry that their answers can be connected to their identity.
<!-- slide-break -->
    - !!! warning "An online survey is not automatically representative"
        - A large number of responses does not fix a poor sample.
        - A public survey link usually produces a volunteer sample.
        - People who notice the link and choose to respond may differ from the target population.
        - Sampling and survey administration are separate decisions.

## Comparing the four [[survey modes]]
- | Survey mode | Interviewer | Presentation | Questionnaire |
  | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
  | Face-to-face | Present | Visual and auditory | Can be long and complex |
  | Telephone | Present | Mainly auditory | Must use short questions and response lists |
  | Postal | Absent | Visual | Must be completely self-explanatory |
  | Online | Absent | Mainly visual | Can use automatic skips and validation |

- | Survey mode | Relative cost | Relative speed | Common threat |
  | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
  | Face-to-face | High | Slow | Interviewer and social desirability effects |
  | Telephone | Medium | Fast | Contact difficulties and auditory limitations |
  | Postal | Low | Slow | Nonresponse and manual processing |
  | Online | Low | Very fast | Coverage, breakoff, and device differences |

<!-- slide-break -->
# [[Mode effect]]
- Survey mode can affect how respondents understand and answer the same question. When the mode changes the answer, this is called a [[mode effect]].
- Respondents may react differently because of:
    - The presence or absence of an interviewer,
    - Whether the question is heard or read,
    - The amount of privacy,
    - The response categories they can see, and
    - The amount of time they have to answer.
- Face-to-face, postal, and online surveys can show response categories.
- Telephone surveys usually present response categories by sound only.
- Visual presentation allows respondents to:
    - Read categories more than once,
    - Compare categories, and
    - Use long lists more easily.
    - Visual lists may produce a [[primacy effect]]:
        - Respondents **may be more likely** to choose categories near the beginning of the list.
    - Auditory lists may produce a [[recency effect]]:
        - Respondents **may be more likely** to choose categories heard near the end of the list.
    - Therefore, the same response list may produce different answers across modes.

# Main [[survey errors]]
- **(1) [[Coverage error]]:** some members of the target population cannot be reached through the sampling frame.
    - Each survey mode can create a different coverage problem:
        - **Face-to-face:** people in inaccessible buildings or distant locations may be difficult to reach.
        - **Telephone:** people without usable contact numbers may be missed.
        - **Postal:** people without stable or accurate addresses may be missed.
        - **Online:** people without reliable internet access or digital skills may be missed.
- **(2) [[Nonresponse error]]:** people who do not respond differ from those who respond.
    - The [[response rate]] is the percentage of eligible sampled people who complete the survey.
        - A higher response rate is desirable.
    - Nonresponse bias occurs when people who do not respond differ in an important way from those who do respond.
    - There are two forms of [[nonresponse error|nonresponse errors]]:
        - **[[Unit nonresponse]]:** a sampled person does not participate at all.
        - **[[Item nonresponse]]:** a respondent participates but skips a particular question.
- **(3) [[Measurement error]]:** the answer recorded is different from the answer the question was intended to measure.
- **(4) [[Processing error]]:** mistakes occur while answers are coded, entered, edited, or prepared for analysis.
